# The skeleton test: is the compressed proof closer to the "essence"?

Word counts can't tell a proof that got clearer from one that got denser. So this test strips the compressed
`Solution.lean` (commit `af85115`, 53,722 words) down to its **statements**: every declaration after the protected
preamble, with proofs and certificate data removed. Then it asks whether those statements read as the argument of
the paper (`paper/PAPER.md`). If they do, the proof's structure is legible even where its insides are not.

The skeleton is in [`SKELETON.lean.txt`](SKELETON.lean.txt), generated by `formal/lean/scripts/skeleton.py`.

"Checker infrastructure" is the machinery that lets the kernel check certificates: polynomial expressions and the
Kronecker substitution (`Kron`, `Ex`), the certificate checkers (`Cert`, `Blk`, `Cert3`, `TCert`), the 1D Bernstein
checker (`SlabOneD`, `CutOneD`) and the contact certificates (`Glue.Coerce`). In the paper all of this is one
section, §8, which describes how the kernel checks certificates.

## Findings

1. **The mathematical spine survived and is legible.** About 1,700 words of statements, a third of the paper's
   length, tell the same story in the same order. The statements of Case 1, closeness, rigidity and the final
   assembly read almost like the paper's sentences.
2. **The certificates and the checker are the alien part, as predicted.** They are about 17k words of numbers plus
   about 5k words of infrastructure. Neither has any counterpart in the paper's argument beyond "the kernel checks
   these". This matches Wolfram's computational irreducibility point: the compression shrank this part, but nothing
   about it became more understandable.
3. **Compression removed named steps.** About 20 declarations that the paper or the theorem map cites by name are
   gone, inlined into larger proofs. Among them are the minimal-pair reduction (`exists_minpair_perm`), the
   local-minimality chain (`pent_local_min`, `local_ineq`), `three_point_bound`, `capSpec_sound` and
   `slabSpec_sound`. The steps still exist, but as anonymous parts of a bigger proof. The skeleton therefore has
   fewer, coarser claims than the paper: this is the minified-JavaScript effect at the level of structure.
4. **The metric caused part of that.** A named lemma costs its statement's words, so inlining single-use lemmas
   always lowers the word count, even when the lemma was a meaningful step. Word count rewards removing exactly the
   intermediate claims a human narrative needs.

## Verdict

**Somewhere in between,** as the README guessed. Measured by statements, the proof is close to its essence at the
top level: a short readable spine, plus two typed-bound statements that only make sense next to the paper.
Underneath, the certificates and the checker stay alien, and some meaningful intermediate steps were folded away by
an objective that counts their names as cost.

If the goal shifts from "shortest" to "most comprehensible", the metric should change too. For example, count only
proof words and not statement words, or require one named lemma for each step of the paper's outline. Under that
metric, the right next move would be to restore a handful of named steps, not to remove more.
